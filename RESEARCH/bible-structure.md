# How the Bible is organised (architecture only)

Status: **Research note.** Purpose: understand the structural conventions The Teryn may borrow, adapt or reject. Not concerned with religious teaching.

## 1. It is a library, not a book

- "Bible" comes from the Greek *ta biblia*, "the books". It is an anthology of many works by many (mostly anonymous) hands, composed and edited over many centuries, in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek.
- Christian Bibles have two major divisions: the **Old Testament** (largely the Jewish scriptures) and the **New Testament**.
- The Jewish Bible (the **Tanakh**) groups the same material into three sections: **Torah** (Teaching), **Nevi'im** (Prophets) and **Ketuvim** (Writings).

## 2. The canon differs between traditions

Which books are "in" is a decision made by communities over time, not a fixed fact.

| Tradition | Books | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish Tanakh | 24 | Same content as the Protestant Old Testament, but counted differently (e.g. the twelve minor prophets count as one book). |
| Protestant | 66 | 39 Old Testament + 27 New Testament. |
| Roman Catholic | 73 | Adds the "deuterocanonical" books (e.g. Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch). |
| Eastern Orthodox / Ethiopian | more | Further books again; the Ethiopian canon is the largest. |

Books outside a tradition's canon but historically associated with it are often called **apocrypha**.

**Lesson for The Teryn:** a collection needs an explicit, published process for deciding what is in it. Ambiguity here causes lasting division.

## 3. Books

- Books vary enormously in length. Psalms has 150 chapters; Obadiah, Philemon, Jude, 2 John and 3 John have one chapter each.
- Book names come from different sources: a supposed author (*Isaiah*), a main character (*Ruth*, *Job*), the subject (*Numbers*, *Lamentations*), the opening word or theme (*Genesis*, meaning "origin"), or the recipients (*Romans*, a letter to Christians in Rome).
- Some books come in numbered pairs or sets (*1 Kings*, *2 Kings*; *1, 2, 3 John*).
- Several person-named books were attributed to that person by tradition, and many modern scholars think the attribution is doubtful. A person's name on a book was never a guarantee of authorship.

## 4. Chapters and verses were added much later

The original authors wrote neither chapters nor verses. The numbering is a **reference grid laid over the text afterwards**.

- **Chapters:** the system in use today is attributed to **Stephen Langton** (d. 1228), later Archbishop of Canterbury, in the early 13th century.
- **Old Testament verses:** Hebrew manuscripts already marked sentence ends (Masoretic tradition, c. 10th century). Verse *numbers* derive chiefly from **Rabbi Isaac Nathan ben Kalonymus**'s Hebrew concordance, c. 1440s.
- **New Testament verses:** numbered by the Paris printer **Robert Estienne (Stephanus)** in his 1551 Greek New Testament. His 1555 Latin Bible was the first with numbered verses throughout, integrated into the text.
- The **Geneva Bible (1560)** was the first complete English Bible numbered throughout.

Because the numbering was imposed later, chapter breaks sometimes fall in awkward places, mid-argument or mid-story.

**Lesson for The Teryn:** we can design the numbering deliberately from the start, alongside the writing, and avoid the Bible's accidents.

## 5. Scale

- Protestant Bible (King James Version): **1,189 chapters, about 31,100 verses, about 780,000 words.**
- Average: about **26 verses per chapter** and about **25 words per verse**.
- Longest chapter: Psalm 119 (176 verses). Shortest: Psalm 117 (2 verses).

## 6. How references work

- Standard form: **Book Chapter:Verse**, e.g. *John 3:16* (book John, chapter 3, verse 16).
- Ranges: *John 3:16–18*; across chapters: *Genesis 1:1–2:3*.
- Single-chapter books are often cited by verse alone: *Jude 3*.
- Standard abbreviations: *Gen*, *Exod*, *Ps*, *1 Cor*.
- Translation or edition is added when wording matters: *John 3:16 (KJV)*.
- Digital standards use machine identifiers, e.g. OSIS uses `John.3.16`.

## 7. Numbering problems that never go away

- **Different traditions number differently.** Psalms 9–147 are numbered one lower in Greek and Latin tradition than in Hebrew and Protestant Bibles. Hebrew Bibles sometimes count a psalm's heading as verse 1. English *Malachi 4* is *Malachi 3:19–24* in Hebrew Bibles.
- **Removed verses leave gaps.** Modern translations based on older manuscripts omit some verses found in later ones (e.g. *Matthew 18:11*, *Acts 8:37*). They keep the gap, or relegate the verse to a footnote, rather than renumber everything after it.

**Lesson for The Teryn:** once a numbering is public, never renumber. Retired verses keep their numbers. This precedent is directly useful for a living collection.

## 8. Literary forms

The Bible's variety is one of its structural strengths:

- **Narrative and history:** stories of origins, families, kings (*Genesis*, *Samuel*, *Kings*).
- **Law:** codes and rules (*Leviticus*, *Deuteronomy*).
- **Poetry and song:** *Psalms*; erotic love poetry in the *Song of Songs*.
- **Lament:** grief poetry (*Lamentations*).
- **Wisdom literature:**
  - *Proverbs*: short sayings.
  - *Ecclesiastes*: a sceptical, world-weary meditation on futility and enjoying life while you have it.
  - *Job*: a dramatic poetic dialogue about undeserved suffering, in which the conventional explanations offered by the friends are rejected.
- **Prophecy:** speeches of warning and social criticism.
- **Gospels:** four overlapping, sometimes conflicting accounts of one life. This is a structural precedent for multiple perspectives on the same subject.
- **Letters (epistles):** addressed to communities or individuals.
- **Apocalyptic:** visionary writing about the end of the world (*Revelation*).
- **Genealogies, lists, parables.**

The overall arc runs from the beginning of the world (*Genesis*) to its end (*Revelation*).

## 9. Physical presentation conventions

- **Two layouts.**
  - *Verse-per-paragraph*: each verse starts a new line (King James tradition). It is good for reference but breaks up the prose.
  - *Paragraph format*: normal paragraphs with small superscript verse numbers (most modern translations). It reads like ordinary prose.
- Poetry is set in indented lines.
- **Section headings** inside chapters are added by modern editors. They are not part of the text and not used in references.
- **Apparatus:** cross-references (margin or centre column), footnotes on translation, concordances (an index of every word), maps, reading plans, study notes.
- Many editions exist (pocket, study, large-print, parallel translations, "red-letter").
